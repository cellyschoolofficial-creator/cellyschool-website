# 7Kb Springs — voice script

**Check before recording (flags)**
- ⚑ **Hooke's anagram** (hook, Kb00; true story, Kb10): the book says only that Hooke's Law is named after Robert Hooke (1635–1703), who studied how metals stretch, that his work led to the force meter, and that the unit of force is named after his "competitor and enemy" Isaac Newton. The anagram is ◇, beyond the book. In 1676 Hooke printed the letters "ceiiinosssttuv" and revealed the answer in 1678: "ut tensio, sic vis", "as the extension, so the force". Some books print a "u" where the script's letters have a "v" (Latin spelling); either is fine. The script reads the letters out in groups and says "about two years".
- ⚑ **Hooke named the cell** (Kb10_09): ◇. In his book *Micrographia* (1665), Hooke used the word "cell" for the tiny boxes he saw in cork. Well documented. It links to 7Aa and to Celly School's name.
- ⚑ **Modelling clay as a plastic material** (Kb05): the book asks the child to "name one plastic material" but doesn't give one. Modelling clay is the usual answer ◇.
- **Spring numbers:** book Q4 (a spring stretches 2 cm with 10 N, so 4 cm with 20 N). The 10 cm → 13 cm example in Kb03 is made up for practice and is not a book figure.
- **Book facts used:** stretched (longer) or compressed (shorter); depends on the material and the size of the force; springs are coils of wire; extension = the difference between the original length and the stretched length; elastic = returns to its original length; Hooke's Law, proportional, every 1 N gives the same stretch, the limit of proportionality; the elastic limit, plastic materials; bungee cords are elastic but don't follow Hooke's Law; Xpogo jumps up to three metres; force meters have springs; 0–10 N and 0–50 N meters; Hooke, the force meter, Newton.
- **Safety (home lab):** wear glasses or sunglasses, never overstretch the band, keep faces away from a stretched band, and use light coins only.
- ◇ **"Extension is extra"**, **"Double the force, double the stretch"** and **"Elastic escapes back. Plastic stays put."** are Volty-made tricks.

Pearson Exploring Science International, Year 7, Student Book 7Kb Springs (p168–169). Celly School's second lesson of 7K Forces.
- **Size:** 136 spoken lines (Volty 126, Bolt 10), 23 long pauses, 13 scenes (hook + 12), 2 parts, about 1370 words. That is roughly 16–17 minutes with pauses.
- **Host:** Volty the spark (voice Autonoe).
- **Sidekick:** Bolt the robot (voice Puck).
- **Sentence length:** 15 words or fewer.

**Lesson grammar:** same as 7Ia.

**World:** The Pogo and Trampoline Pit in the Arrow Arena. Xpogo athletes bounce on spring sticks. Every spring hangs beside a giant painted ruler. Each time a 1 N weight clinks on, the spring drops by the same step. At the far end of the pit is a red line on the floor: the Point of No Return (the elastic limit). Newt the referee watches from his high chair.
- **Case:** "The case of the secret jumble" (the 7Ka teaser).
- **Answered at the end:** Robert Hooke hid his law of springs inside an anagram, so nobody could steal it. The letters spelled a Latin sentence: "As the extension, so the force." Double the force, double the stretch.

**Main tricks:**
- "Extension is extra." Pinch the gap between the old length and the new length.
- "Double the force, double the stretch." Pull one hand away from the other, then twice as far.
- "Elastic escapes back. Plastic stays put." Squish an imaginary ball, let it spring back; squish it again and keep it squashed.
