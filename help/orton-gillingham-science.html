Teaching science the multisensory, Orton-Gillingham way

Orton-Gillingham is best known for teaching reading, but its principles work well for science: teach one small idea at a time, use several senses together (see, hear, say, touch, move), build on what is already secure, and review often. In practice that means short parts, words tapped in beats, hands-on activities and frequent quick recall.

The principles, applied to science

  1. Structured: follow a clear order and teach one idea per step, for example cell, then tissue, then organ, then system.
  2. Multisensory: pair the picture with the spoken word, the tapped beats and a gesture or model.
  3. Cumulative: start each session with a quick recall of last time before adding anything new.
  4. Explicit: say exactly what a word means and show it; don't leave children to guess from text.
  5. Diagnostic: notice what did not stick and come back to it next time.

Example: cells, tissues and organs for an 11-year-old

See it: a simple picture of a cell, then many cells joined into a tissue.

Say and tap it: tis·sue (2 beats), or·gan (2 beats), or·gan sys·tem (4 beats).

Move it: build a model with building bricks, one brick per cell, a wall for a tissue, a house for an organ.

Recall it: thumbs up or thumbs down to quick true-or-false questions.

Questions parents ask

Is Orton-Gillingham only for reading?

It was designed for reading and spelling, but its core ideas (structured, multisensory, cumulative, explicit) can be used for any subject with a lot of new vocabulary, including science.

What does multisensory science look like at home?

Watching a short explanation, saying and tapping the new words, drawing or building a model, and answering a few quick questions out loud, all in one short session.

Do I need training to use these ideas?

No. Formal Orton-Gillingham tutoring needs training, but parents can use the simple principles: one idea at a time, several senses together, and lots of short reviews.
